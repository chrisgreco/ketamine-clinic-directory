## IV Ketamine vs Spravato (Esketamine): What's the Difference?

If you are exploring ketamine therapy for depression, you have likely encountered two primary options: IV racemic ketamine and Spravato (esketamine) nasal spray. While both are related compounds, they differ significantly in their molecular structure, FDA status, cost, insurance coverage, and how they are administered.

Understanding these differences is essential for making an informed decision about your treatment. This comparison guide breaks down the key distinctions to discuss with your healthcare provider.

**Important:** This comparison is for educational purposes only. The right treatment depends on your individual medical situation, insurance coverage, and provider recommendations. Always consult your physician before making treatment decisions.

## The Molecular Difference

Racemic ketamine contains two mirror-image molecules: S-ketamine (esketamine) and R-ketamine (arketamine). These two forms, called enantiomers, interact with brain receptors somewhat differently.

Spravato contains only the S-ketamine enantiomer. Research suggests S-ketamine has a higher affinity for the NMDA receptor, meaning it binds more strongly at that target. Some researchers believe R-ketamine may also contribute meaningful therapeutic effects through different pathways, including opioid receptors and AMPA receptors. This has led some clinicians to prefer racemic ketamine, arguing it provides a broader range of mechanisms.

However, the clinical significance of this molecular difference remains an active area of research. Both forms have demonstrated antidepressant effects in published studies.

## FDA Approval and Off-Label Use

**Spravato** received FDA approval in March 2019 for treatment-resistant depression (TRD) and was later approved for major depressive disorder with suicidal ideation (MDSI). This means it has gone through rigorous Phase III clinical trials specifically for these indications.

**IV racemic ketamine** has FDA approval as an anesthetic agent but is used off-label for depression and other psychiatric conditions. Off-label prescribing is legal and common in medicine, but it means the specific psychiatric use has not completed the formal FDA approval process. Some clinicians argue that the evidence base for IV racemic ketamine is at least as strong as for Spravato, citing decades of research and clinical experience.

## Insurance and Cost

The FDA approval status of Spravato is its most significant practical advantage: insurance coverage. Because Spravato has an approved indication, it has established billing codes and is covered by many commercial insurance plans, Medicare Part B, and some Medicaid programs. Out-of-pocket costs with insurance may be as low as $10 to $150 per session, though this varies by plan.

IV racemic ketamine is rarely covered by insurance because it is used off-label. Most patients pay the full $400 to $800 per session out of pocket. Some clinics have found ways to bill associated services (monitoring, psychiatric evaluation) through insurance, which can reduce costs.

The manufacturer of Spravato (Janssen/Johnson & Johnson) also offers a patient savings program that may further reduce copays for eligible commercially insured patients.

## Administration and Experience

**IV ketamine** is administered through an intravenous line over 40 to 60 minutes. The dose is calculated based on body weight (typically 0.5 mg/kg) and can be precisely adjusted during the infusion. This allows providers to fine-tune the dose to each patient's response. The dissociative effects during IV infusion can be moderate to significant.

**Spravato** is self-administered as a nasal spray under clinical supervision. The patient sprays the device into each nostril at five-minute intervals. The dosing is fixed at either 56 mg or 84 mg, with less flexibility for individual adjustment. The dissociative effects are generally reported as milder than IV ketamine, though this varies by individual.

Both treatments require in-clinic administration. Spravato has a mandatory two-hour observation period under the REMS program, while IV ketamine observation periods vary by clinic (typically 30 to 60 minutes post-infusion).

## REMS Program Requirements

Spravato is subject to a Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) program, meaning:

- It can only be administered in certified healthcare settings
- Patients must be monitored for at least two hours after each session
- Patients must be enrolled in the REMS registry
- The medication cannot be dispensed to patients for at-home use
- Providers and pharmacies must be REMS-certified

IV racemic ketamine does not have REMS requirements, though responsible clinics implement their own safety monitoring protocols that are often comparable.

## Which Is Right for You?

The decision between IV ketamine and Spravato depends on several factors:

- **Insurance coverage:** If your insurance covers Spravato and you qualify, it may be significantly more affordable.
- **Dose flexibility:** If your provider wants precise dose control, IV ketamine offers more adjustability.
- **Access:** Spravato is available only at REMS-certified locations. IV ketamine can be administered at any licensed medical facility with appropriate capabilities.
- **Provider recommendation:** Your physician's clinical judgment, based on your specific situation, is the most important factor.

Discuss both options with your healthcare provider to determine the best approach for your individual needs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I switch from Spravato to IV ketamine or vice versa?

Yes, some patients switch between the two based on their response, insurance changes, or provider recommendations. Your clinician can help determine if switching is appropriate. There is generally no required waiting period between the two treatments.

### Is one more effective than the other?

Head-to-head comparison data is limited. Some clinicians report that IV racemic ketamine may produce stronger effects in certain patients, while others respond well to Spravato. Published research has not definitively established one as superior. Individual response varies significantly.

### Can I receive Spravato at any ketamine clinic?

No. Spravato can only be administered at REMS-certified locations. Not all ketamine clinics have this certification. If you are interested in Spravato specifically, confirm REMS certification before scheduling.

### Why would a clinic offer IV ketamine instead of Spravato?

Some providers prefer IV ketamine because of the dose flexibility, the broader molecular profile of racemic ketamine, and the ability to treat conditions beyond depression (such as chronic pain, PTSD, and anxiety). The REMS requirements for Spravato also add administrative burden that some practices choose not to take on.

### Is Spravato safer than IV ketamine?

Both treatments have similar safety profiles when administered in appropriate clinical settings. Spravato has the advantage of formal FDA safety monitoring through the REMS program. IV ketamine has a longer history of clinical use at sub-anesthetic doses. Neither is inherently safer or more dangerous than the other when used as directed.
